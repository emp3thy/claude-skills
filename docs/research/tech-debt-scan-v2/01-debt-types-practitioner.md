Research note for tech-debt-scan v2. Compiled 2026-09-02. Companion to the academic-taxonomy note (02). The downstream consumer is an LLM-driven, read-only repository scanner, so every type is described in terms of what a scanner can see in files, manifests, CI config and git history.

## 3. Prevalence ranking rationale

Only one source ranks debt types by frequency within a single taxonomy: Google's quarterly survey, whose ten categories are listed "in the order of most to least frequently reported as a hindrance" (G23). The authors warn that this ordering reflects Google's monorepo and tooling and "will not generalize"; dependency debt in particular is suppressed there. Every other source measures either presence (share of organisations, applications, services or downloads showing the problem) or pain (share of developers naming it as frustration, or hours lost).

The ranking above therefore uses three keys in order. First, breadth of artefact-level evidence that the type exists in a typical codebase: measurements of real dependencies, applications and commits (DD26, SONA23, SONA24, VERA26, GC25, GC26, GH17) outrank self-report. Second, Google's frequency ordering, adjusted where Google itself says its environment is atypical. Third, practitioner emphasis across the engineering-blog and consultancy sources.

Most frequently present (evidence-backed): code quality cruft, dependency and upgrade debt, documentation, test debt, security debt, migration debt, duplication and AI-generated code, dead code. Each has either a near-universal presence measure (87 percent of organisations, 93 percent of respondents, 82 percent of organisations, 80 percent of unmanaged dependencies) or a top-five Google rank.

Most costly or painful per practitioner reports (a different ordering): migration debt is Google's number one hindrance; architectural and coupling debt is reported as the most damaging form (VF24) and drives the 15x defect and 124 percent time-in-development gap (CR22); code-quality hotspots carry the interest, since interest is only paid where code is touched (FOW-TD); test debt shows up as rollbacks and flakiness; knowledge debt shows up as onboarding failure (TW-5); security debt carries breach risk rather than developer time.

Where evidence is thin: observability, data and schema, ML, IaC, tooling, unused features and performance debt are supported by practitioner narrative and a single company taxonomy each, with no cross-organisation presence numbers. Their positions at ranks 14 to 22 reflect that absence, not proof of rarity. Two "presence" numbers deserve caution: Stripe's 2018 figures are self-estimates of hours, and Stepsize's 2021 sample is about 200 people.

## 4. Symptom-detectability assessment

Columns: (a) static reading of source, (b) git-history mining, (c) manifest, config and CI inspection, (d) requires runtime or CI data, (e) requires human knowledge. Y = primary channel, P = partial or corroborating, N = not usable.

| Dependency and upgrade | P (deprecated calls) | P (last bump dates) | Y (versions, EOL, pins) | N | N |

| Dead / abandoned code | Y (unreferenced symbols) | Y (untouched files) | P (unused deps) | P (usage data) | P |

| Feature flags / config | Y (flag checks, dead branches) | Y (flag age) | P (flag definitions) | Y (rollout state) | P |
| Tooling / dev environment | P (scripts) | N | Y (setup files, lockfiles) | N | Y (does it hurt) |
| Process / ownership | N | Y (stale branches, TODO age) | Y (CODEOWNERS, templates) | N | Y |
| Data / schema | Y (migrations, models) | P (hand-edited migrations) | P | P (data quality) | Y |
| ML / AI system | Y (notebooks, glue, binaries) | P (experiment branches) | P (data versioning) | Y (drift) | P |
| Observability / logging | Y (print calls, empty catches) | N | Y (logging, tracing libs absent) | Y (alert noise) | P |
| Unused features | P (no callers) | Y (no activity) | N | Y (usage) | Y |
| Performance / reliability | P (N+1, sync IO, no timeouts) | N | P | Y | P |

For an LLM read-only scanner the highest-yield channels are (a) plus (b) for ranks 1, 6, 7, 8, 10 and 15, and (c) alone for ranks 2, 5, 12, 13 and 14. Google's own attempt to predict survey-reported debt from 117 code and process metrics is a warning: linear models explained under 1 percent of variance, and random forests reached over 80 percent precision but only 10 to 25 percent recall (G23). Repo signals find real debt but miss most of what engineers report, because much debt is a gap between the present and a desired state that lives in people's heads.

## 5. Open questions and evidence gaps

- No public dataset ranks debt types by frequency across many organisations; Google's ordering is the only within-taxonomy ranking and is explicitly company-specific. A cross-company replication of the G23 survey instrument would resolve the top-ten ordering.
- Practitioner surveys conflate "tech debt" into one item (62.4 percent in SO24, 41 percent in SONAR26), so they say how much debt hurts but not which kind. The Sonar 2026 full report may contain a breakdown but is behind a download form.
- McKinsey and Gartner figures are widely repeated but could not be fetched from the primary source here; treat the percentages as unverified until the report PDF is read.
- Observability, data/schema, IaC, tooling and ML debt lack any presence measurement. The scanner can still emit these findings, but confidence weights should reflect the thin base.
- The AI-code numbers (GC25, GC26, SONAR26) are from vendors with products in the area; they are measurements of real commits but selection of repositories is not fully described. Independent replication would strengthen rank 7.
- Feature-flag prevalence rests on one company (Uber) and one secondary citation; a broader measurement of stale-flag age in open-source repos would help calibrate the 90-day heuristic.
- Whether "cognitive debt" (TW-COG) is a distinct type or a symptom of knowledge and duplication debt is unsettled; it is folded into ranks 7 and 10 here.
